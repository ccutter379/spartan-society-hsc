The rules

Your booklet is the only thing you may bring into Task 1

Your annotations are also your process evidence. Your teacher sights them in class before the task and checks them against your answers afterwards.

✓ You may

  • Write by hand, in pen or pencil, in your own words.
  • Annotate in class, during the lessons when we study each source.
  • Underline, highlight, circle and use arrows.
  • Use short notes, codes and symbols.
  • Use AI only to revise before the task, for example to quiz yourself on terms and writers.

✗ You may not

  • Use printed, typed, pasted or photocopied notes.
  • Add sticky notes, loose sheets or extra pages.
  • Copy AI-generated or AI-rewritten notes into the booklet.
  • Copy another student’s annotations or a model answer.
  • Write whole answers to guessed questions. Annotate the sources.

The codes

Work through O, V, P, R, L, in that order

O

Origin. Who made it, when and where? Written or archaeological? Primary or secondary? How close to the events?

V

Value. What does it show about Spartan society? Which dot point, which group? What can it tell us that other sources can’t?

P

Purpose. Why was it made? Who was it for? How might that shape what it says?

R

Reliability. Can we trust it for this? What strengthens or weakens it? Does another source agree or disagree?

L

Limitations. What can’t it tell us? Whose voices are missing? Is it fragmentary, late or translated?

Source A · I do

Herodotus on Lykourgos and Delphi

Annotations

  • OHdt, c. 430s–420s BC — earliest mention of Lyk., but 200+ yrs after the reformsHerodotus is the earliest writer we have who names Lykourgos. But he wrote two centuries or more after the reforms are usually dated, so he is reporting tradition.
  • VSpartans credit ONE man with it all: laws, army, messes, ephors, gerousia (Sp)By Herodotus’ day the Spartans already gave one founder credit for almost every institution, and honoured him with a temple. That is evidence for the Lykourgos tradition, not necessarily for Lykourgos himself.
  • Pexplains how Sparta went from worst-governed → ‘excellent government’Herodotus is explaining why King Croesus of Lydia wanted Sparta, now strong and well ordered, as an ally. He reports what he was told.
  • R✓ gives 2 versions (Delphi / Crete) + names the Spartans’ own · ✗ oracle verse may be shaped laterHerodotus openly gives two versions and says which one the Spartans themselves told. That is a strength. But verse oracles like this one circulated by word of mouth and could be shaped after the event.
  • Loral tradition — no eyewitness, no date for Lyk.Herodotus relied on what people told him. No one alive had seen Lykourgos, and the source gives no date for him.
  • GSp + a king (Leobotas) only · no He, Pe or WOnly Spartiates and one king appear. There is nothing from helots, perioikoi or women.
  • ★syssitia (messes), ephors, gerousiaThese are the institutions the source links to Lykourgos. Starring them makes the source quick to find for a question on government.
  • →→ B ✗ Tyrtaios: Apollo + kings, no Lyk. · → N Nafissi: the legend grewTyrtaios (Source B), a Spartan poet of the 7th century BC, credits Apollo’s oracle and the kings and never names Lykourgos. Nafissi (Source N) argues early Spartans saw Lykourgos as an organiser of political life, not yet Herodotus’ reformer who changed everything.

Source B · I do

The Great Rhetra, in Plutarch and Tyrtaios

Annotations

  • OA: Plut., c. AD 100 (~700 yrs later, prob. via Aristotle) · B: Tyrtaios, mid-7th C BC — a Spartan at the time!Two very different origins. Plutarch wrote about 700 years after the Rhetra and probably took its text from Aristotle’s lost work on the Spartan constitution. Tyrtaios is the only Spartan voice from the time.
  • Vfounding rules: gerousia of 30 (28 + 2 kings), assembly, people decide → rider lets elders + kings dissolve itIt keeps the wording of Sparta’s founding document: kings, elders and people. The rider then let the elders and kings throw out a ‘crooked’ decision of the people.
  • PPlut.: Lyk. = ideal lawgiver · Tyrt.: Apollo (Phoibos) gives the order divine authorityPlutarch’s Life presents Lykourgos as the ideal lawgiver. Tyrtaios’ poem gave the system Apollo’s authority at a time of war and unrest.
  • R✓ odd, archaic wording → genuine early text · ✗ came via Aristotle + Plut.; text damagedThe old, obscure wording suggests a genuine early document. But it reached us through Aristotle and Plutarch, and the text is damaged in places.
  • LPlut. credits Lyk.; Tyrt. credits Delphi + kings, never Lyk. → they disagree! · no ephorsThe two extracts disagree on who made the constitution. Tyrtaios survives only where later writers quote him. The Rhetra also never mentions the ephors.
  • Gkings, elders, the People (Sp) · no Pe, He or WOnly the kings, the elders and the citizen body (the damos) appear. Perioikoi, helots and women have no place in the constitution.
  • ★gerousia, ekklesia (= Apella), archagetai = kings, rhetraPlutarch explains the old words himself: the ‘founder-leaders’ are the kings, and to ‘have an Apella’ is to call an assembly of the people.
  • →→ A ✗ Hdt credits Lyk. · → C ✓ 28 + 2 kings = 30 · → D gerousia later took bribesHerodotus (Source A) credits Lykourgos. Herodotus (Source C) says the kings sit with the 28 members of the gerousia, which matches a gerousia of 30. Aristotle (Source D) says elders later took bribes.

Source C · I do

Herodotus on the kings’ privileges and funerals

Annotations

  • OHdt, c. 430s–420s BC · non-Spartan using informants · living memory of LeonidasHerodotus was not Spartan and relied on informants, but he wrote within living memory of kings such as Leonidas and Leotychidas.
  • Vfullest list of kings’ privileges: religious, military, legal, political + royal funeralsNo other source lists so many royal privileges: two priesthoods of Zeus, leading the army with a bodyguard of 100, deciding who marries an unbetrothed heiress, and a seat with the gerousia.
  • Pwritten for Greek readers — Spartan customs he found unusualHerodotus wrote for other Greeks and picked out customs he found unusual. In the part left out of this extract, he compares the funerals with ‘barbarian’ Asian ones.
  • R✓ detailed, fits Xenophon · ✗ no proof kings declared war alone; Thuc. denies ‘two votes’Much of it fits Xenophon’s account. But Cooley notes there is no evidence kings could declare war on their own, and Thucydides (1.20) denies the ‘two votes’ claim.
  • Lrules on paper, not practice · outsider · silent on ephors checking kingsIt lists the rules, not how they worked in practice, and says nothing about the ephors’ checks on the kings (Source D).
  • Gkings, Sp, Pe, He, W (basins, lament) → funeral binds every group to the kingsSpartiates and perioikoi had to attend; helots were there and mourned too, though Herodotus doesn’t say they were forced. Hodkinson (2026) reads this as a sign helots were ‘partially integrated’ into Spartiate society.
  • ★gerousia, perioikoi, helots · Pythians (NOT ‘pithoi’)The envoys to Delphi are the ‘Pythians’. One textbook misprints this as ‘pithoi’, which are storage jars.
  • →→ K twin gods ↔ two kings · → D ✗ kings mistrusted · → F ✓ Messenians mourn mastersThe Dioskouroi relief (Source K) links twin gods with the two kings. Aristotle (Source D) says the lawgiver mistrusted the kings. Tyrtaios (Source F) says the conquered Messenians had to mourn their masters.

Source D · I do

Aristotle on the ephors, the gerousia and the kings

Annotations

  • OAristotle, 330s–320s BC · philosopher in Athens · Sparta weak after LeuctraAristotle was a philosopher from Stagira who worked in Athens. He wrote in the 330s–320s BC, when Sparta had become a second-rate power after its defeat at Leuctra in 371 BC.
  • Vwhy it held together: kings (honour), elders (prize), people (ephorate) · kings kept at loggerheadsAristotle explains how the constitution held together: each group had a share. This is his ‘mixed constitution’ argument for the balance of kings, gerousia and ephorate.
  • Pjudges constitutions → hunting for faults (‘bad’, ‘not good’, ‘childish’)The Politics compares constitutions to explain why states fail, so Aristotle is looking for faults. He also wrote a lost work on the Spartan constitution.
  • R✓ specific: bribes, ‘childish’ elections (shouting) · ✗ hindsight after Sparta’s fall; a theoristHe gives specific details, such as elders taking bribes and being chosen in a ‘childish’ way (by shouting, as Plutarch describes). But he wrote with hindsight after Sparta’s collapse, as a theorist, and Cooley calls some of his interpretation ‘extreme’.
  • L4th C Sparta, not the early system · nothing on how the ekklesia worked · hostileHe describes Sparta in his own century, not necessarily the archaic system. He says nothing directly about how the assembly worked, and his philosophical frame is hostile.
  • Gephors often ‘extremely poor men’, elders ‘the great and the good’, kings, people (Sp) · no He, Pe or WCitizens of very different wealth shared office: poor men could become ephors, while the elders were ‘the great and the good’. Helots, perioikoi and women do not appear.
  • ★ephors, gerousia, kings · ‘mixed constitution’Aristotle is the key source for the ‘mixed constitution’ of kings, gerousia and ephorate. A similar Aristotle extract appeared in the 2022 HSC (Question 11(d)).
  • →→ B Rhetra’s gerousia · → C ✗ kings honoured vs mistrusted · → E ephors vs Kinadon · → I ephors declare war on helotsThe Rhetra (Source B) sets up the gerousia Aristotle criticises. Herodotus (Source C) shows kings honoured, while Aristotle says they were mistrusted. The ephors deal with Kinadon’s conspiracy (Source E), and Plutarch, citing Aristotle, says the ephors declared war on the helots (Source I).

Source E · I do

Xenophon on the conspiracy of Kinadon

Annotations

  • OXenophon, 1st half of 4th C BC · Athenian exile, friend of King Agesilaos · alive at the time (c. 399 BC)Xenophon was an Athenian exile, a soldier and a friend of King Agesilaos, living on an estate Sparta gave him. He was alive when the plot happened, in about 399 BC.
  • Vevery non-Sp group named: He, neodamodeis, Inf, Pe → ‘eat them even raw’ · Kinadon: ‘no one’s inferior’It is the only source that names the hypomeiones, and it shows every non-Spartiate group side by side. It also gives Kinadon’s own motive: “To be no one’s inferior”.
  • Pnarrative history, broadly pro-Spartan → shows the ephors acting fastXenophon’s history also shows how efficient the Spartan authorities were under Agesilaos. He is broadly pro-Spartan.
  • R✓ detailed, contemporary · ✗ informer’s report of a plotter’s words → 40 v 4,000 + hatred exaggerated?It is detailed and written at the time. But it is an informer’s report of what a conspirator said, and Kinadon had every reason to exaggerate the numbers and the other groups’ hatred (Cooley). The Spartan state stayed stable until 370/69 BC.
  • Lother groups heard only via a hostile report + pro-Spartan writer · no He or Pe voiceWe hear the inferior groups only through a hostile report and a pro-Spartan narrator. Hodkinson (2026) notes there is no sign the helots were actually involved before the plot was crushed.
  • GSp (homoioi), Inf, neodamodeis (freed He), He, Pe + king, ephors, gerousia · iron-works = Pe craftsThis is the richest single source for the groups in Spartan society. The iron-works link the perioikoi’s crafts to weapons.
  • ★homoioi, hypomeiones, neodamodeis, ephors, perioikoi‘Hypomeiones’ appears only here in all Greek literature (Cooley), so this is the source to cite for the ‘inferiors’.
  • →→ O ✓ Figueira: Kinadon, ‘inferiors’ dangerous · → D ✓ ephors’ great power · → J ✓ fear of helotsFigueira (Source O) uses Kinadon to show the ‘inferiors’ were dangerous. Aristotle (Source D) describes the ephors’ great power, which we see in action here. Thucydides (Source J) shows how far Sparta feared the helots.

Source F · I do

Tyrtaios on the conquered Messenians

Annotations

  • OTyrtaios, mid-7th C BC Spartan poet · during/after the Messenian wars · survives via Pausanias (2nd C AD)Tyrtaios was a Spartan poet writing during or soon after the Messenian wars. His lines survive only because Pausanias quoted them in the 2nd century AD.
  • Vearliest evidence for helots: Messenians hand over HALF their produce + forced to mourn mastersThis is the earliest evidence for helotage, and a Spartan view from the time. Hodkinson (2026) thinks a half share was probably still the arrangement in the classical period.
  • Pwar poetry for Spartans · ‘like asses’ → degrades the conqueredTyrtaios wrote war poetry for a Spartan audience. The simile ‘like asses’ degrades the conquered and celebrates Sparta’s victory.
  • R✓ contemporary + Spartan (rare!) · ✗ poetic, partisan; Pausanias’ Messenian history ‘largely invented’A contemporary Spartan voice is very rare. But this is partisan poetry, and Cooley warns that Pausanias’ history of Messenia, where the lines are quoted, is ‘largely invented’.
  • L2 short fragments, no context · Messenian helots in the 7th C — not Lakonian helots or laterThese are two short fragments out of context. They describe Messenian helots in the 7th century BC, not necessarily Lakonian helots or later periods.
  • GHe (Messenian) + their wives · Spartan masters · no helot voiceThe helots and their wives appear only as the Spartans saw them. We have nothing in their own words.
  • ★helots, Messenia, agriculture (ploughland)Star it for helots and for land and agriculture: the conquered Messenians worked the land and handed over half its produce.
  • →→ C ✓ helots mourn the kings too · → O de Ste. Croix: Messenians = the great danger · → E hatred of mastersHerodotus (Source C) says helots mourned the kings with everyone else, though he says only the free were forced. De Ste. Croix (Source O) argues the Messenian helots were Sparta’s great danger. Kinadon’s informer (Source E) claims the helots hated their masters.

Source G · I do

Xenophon on the education of boys

Annotations

  • OXenophon, 1st half of 4th C BC · Athenian admirer of Sparta, lived under its protection · contemporaryXenophon was an Athenian who admired Sparta and lived under its protection. Plutarch says King Agesilaos urged him to bring his sons up at Sparta.
  • Vearliest detailed account of boys’ training: barefoot, 1 tunic, hunger, stealing, whips → obedience + enduranceIt is the earliest detailed account of the boys’ upbringing, and it shows the values the system aimed at: obedience, endurance and resourcefulness.
  • Ppraises Lyk.: he ‘did the opposite’ of the rest of GreeceThe whole work praises Lykourgos by contrasting Sparta with the rest of Greece. Every section is built as ‘Lykourgos did the opposite’.
  • R✓ well placed, contemporary · ✗ idealises; explains away stealing as training; gives no agesHe was well placed to know, but he idealises the system and explains away awkward features, such as stealing, as deliberate training. He uses age-group names but gives no ages.
  • Lan ideal: nothing on girls, failures or drop-outs · age tables are modern guessesHe describes an ideal and says nothing about girls, failures or boys who dropped out. The ages in modern tables are reconstructions.
  • Gboys (paides), young men (hebontes, eiren), the paidonomos · no girlsBoys, young men and the official in charge appear. Girls are absent from this account.
  • ★agoge, paidonomos, hebontes, eiren, Artemis OrthiaThese are the Spartan names for the people who ran the training. Using them precisely shows detailed knowledge.
  • →→ L ✓ Orthia offerings · → N David: Xen.’s Lyk. looks like Socrates · → P Kennell: late ‘invented traditions’The lead offerings (Source L) come from the same sanctuary as the cheese contest. David (Source N) argues Xenophon shaped Lykourgos to look like Socrates. Kennell (Source P) warns that much later evidence for the training may describe ‘invented traditions’.

Source H · I do

Two accounts of how the army was organised

Annotations

  • OA: Xenophon, 4th C BC, experienced commander · B: Thucydides, Athenian general, alive at Mantineia (418 BC)Xenophon was an experienced commander who lived under Spartan protection. Thucydides was an Athenian general and historian, writing about a battle of his own time while in exile.
  • VA: ephors call up age groups; red cloak, bronze shield; officers in front · B: Spartan secrecy hid their numbersXenophon gives the call-up, the equipment and the officers. Thucydides gives a unit-by-unit estimate and says outright that Spartan secrecy hid their numbers.
  • PA: praises Lyk.’s system as simple + superior · B: best estimate, admits its limitsXenophon wants to show the Lycurgan system is simple and superior. Thucydides wants to give his readers the best estimate he can, while flagging its limits.
  • R✗ the 2 schemes don’t match: 6 morai v. 7 lochoi · ✓ Thuc. admits uncertainty = good methodThe two schemes do not match. Cooley doubts Xenophon fully understood the tactics, and notes Thucydides’ totals are usually thought to leave out the mora. Thucydides admitting uncertainty is itself a sign of good method.
  • Ldifferent dates (418 BC v. 4th C) · maybe counting different things · no totals we can trustThe extracts come from different dates and may count different things, so neither gives totals we can trust. Our translation also leaves out a number: the Greek has eight ‘leaders of 50 men’.
  • GSp hoplites, officers · Skiritai (Pe light-armed) · craftsmen called up tooThe Skiritai were a perioikic light-armed unit, and craftsmen were called up with the army.
  • ★morai, lochoi, enomotiai, polemarch, hoplitesDon’t blend the two schemes into one table. The disagreement is the point, and one textbook makes this mistake.
  • →→ M ✓ a bronze shield · → E iron-works = weapons · → J 700 helot hoplites with BrasidasThe Pylos shield (Source M) matches Xenophon’s ‘bronze shield for every man’. Kinadon’s iron-works (Source E) link crafts to weapons. Thucydides (Source J) shows 700 helots serving as hoplites.

Source I · I do

Plutarch on the krypteia

Annotations

  • OPlut., c. AD 100 · pieced together: Aristotle (lost work) + retells ThucydidesPlutarch was compiling earlier writers: Aristotle’s lost work for the krypteia and the ephors’ declaration of war, and Thucydides for the 2,000 helots.
  • Vfullest account of krypteia: picked young men, daggers, kill helots at night · ephors declare war · helots humiliated in messesIt is the fullest description we have of the krypteia, the annual declaration of war that avoided religious pollution, and the public humiliation of helots in the messes.
  • Pmoral biog. → defends Lyk.: the brutality came ‘later’, after the earthquakePlutarch admires Lykourgos, so he defends him. He doubts the krypteia was Lykourgos’ idea and dates the brutality after the great earthquake (c. 464 BC).
  • R✗ 2nd/3rd-hand, mixes centuries · ✗ changes Thuc.’s story: who picked the helotsPlutarch mixes sources from different centuries. When he retells Thucydides he changes the story: in Thucydides the helots put themselves forward (Source J).
  • Lvery late · no helot voice · purpose debated (Plato: an endurance test)It is very late and has no helot voice. Plato describes the krypteia as an endurance exercise instead. Hodkinson (2026) sets out four views, from helot-hunting as its central purpose to it never happening at all.
  • GHe · selected young Sp · magistrates, ephors · young men watching drunk helotsThe helots appear only as victims and examples. The young Spartiates are chosen, trained and taught by watching.
  • ★krypteia, ephors, syssitia (messes), helotsSome historians date the ephors’ declaration of war after Sparta lost Messenia in 370/69 BC, outside our period. Say so if you use it.
  • →→ J ✗ Thuc.’s original: who picked them · → D ✓ ephors’ power · → P Kennell: Roman-era evidenceCompare Thucydides’ own version (Source J). Aristotle (Source D) describes the ephors’ great power. Kennell (Source P) warns that Roman-era evidence like Plutarch’s may describe later ‘invented traditions’.

Source J · I do

Thucydides on the disappearance of 2,000 helots

Annotations

  • OThuc., late 5th C BC · Athenian, contemporary · exiled 424 BC → heard Peloponnesian informantsThucydides lived through the Peloponnesian War. His exile from 424 BC let him hear informants from the Peloponnese.
  • VSpartan fear, deception + mass killing — yet 700 helots used as hoplitesIt shows fear of the helots, deception and mass killing, and in the same passage Sparta relying on helots as hoplites.
  • Pexplains Spartan policy after Athens took Pylos (425 BC)Thucydides is explaining what Sparta did after the Athenians captured Pylos, and he generalises about Spartan attitudes to the helots.
  • R✓ careful, contemporary · ✗ can’t say how they died; massacre undated; historicity questionedCooley notes it is hard to imagine killing so many without trace, and that the massacre is a different occasion from the 700. Hodkinson (2026) notes its historicity ‘has also been questioned’.
  • Lkey line translates 2 ways: ‘regarding’ v. ‘against’ the helots → how constant was the fear? · no helot voiceThe two bracketed translations are Cooley’s. Translation is interpretation: ‘against the helots’ makes the fear sound constant.
  • GHe (the bravest singled out) · Spartan authorities · Brasidas’ helot hoplitesThe helots who put themselves forward as the bravest are the ones who disappear. Other helots serve Sparta as soldiers.
  • ★helots, hoplites, Pylos, BrasidasPylos and Brasidas fix the date and context: the crisis of 425–424 BC.
  • →→ I ✗ Plut. changes who picked them · → O ✓ de Ste. Croix: helot threat · → P Powell: Spartans boasted · → M PylosPlutarch (Source I) has the Spartans pick the helots. De Ste. Croix (Source O) sees the helot threat as the cardinal fact about Sparta, though Hodkinson finds only one definite revolt, in the 460s. Powell (Source P) suggests Thucydides heard this because Spartans boasted of their deception. The Pylos shield (Source M) comes from the same crisis.
