1 · How Section II works · We do — when there is no glossary verb

No glossary verb? Read the criteria

Other ways Sparta questions begin, 2019–2025
  • What is known about …? — 2021 (b)
  • What does / do … reveal …? — 2019 (b) · 2020 (b) (after “With reference to Source L”) · 2022 (c) · 2023 (c) · 2024 (d)
  • What evidence do … provide …? — 2020 (c)
  • What was the significance …? — 2022 (b)
  • How did Greek writers view …? — 2024 (b)

The glossary itself warns that “HSC exam questions will continue to incorporate self-explanatory terms” such as how, why and to what extent.

Read the top band

2021 (b) has no glossary verb, but its 5-mark criterion asks for “a detailed and accurate description relevant to the question” — so describe (Lesson 16).

Reveal · significance

Treat What does evidence reveal …? as a call to draw conclusions from named evidence, and What was the significance …? as a call to judge importance and consequences.

Cold call: what does explain require that describe does not?

Cause and effect. Describe stops at “characteristics and features”; explain must show why and/or how one thing led to another. NESA’s 2021 feedback on a Pompeii question suggested “using connective language, for example, ‘therefore’ to demonstrate explanation rather than description”.

The same lines in Cooley

This man is bewailed by young and old alike,The whole city is distressed by a grievous sense of loss;His tomb and his children and their children,And all his line thereafter are renowned among men;Never does his good fame perish nor his name,But though he is beneath the earth, he is immortal,Since raging Ares laid him low when he was standing firm,Excelling while fighting for his land and his children;

Tyrtaeus, fragment 12 (West), lines 27–34 · composed mid-7th century BC by a poet who was probably Spartan · trans. K. Hughes · Cooley A9

Quote the paper’s words

The marker reads the translation on your paper. Quote “the whole city mourns him with bitter longing”, not Cooley’s “The whole city is distressed by a grievous sense of loss”.

Same honour, different grammar

Near its end the paper reads “Whoever distinguishes himself in bravery by standing steadfast and fighting”; Cooley gives a reason: “Since raging Ares laid him low when he was standing firm”. Both honour the man who stood firm.

4 · Taking questions apart · I do — the four colours

Before you write a word: four things to mark

1 · Directive verb — what must I do?

The instruction word: outline, describe, explain, analyse, assess. It sets the kind of answer (the verbs table, section 1).

2 · Key terms — about what?

The topic words, read exactly. Myths and legends is plural; a festival is not a myth (Lesson 3).

3 · Scope — where and when?

In this period means Spartan society to the Battle of Leuctra in 371 BC. Use later writers such as Plutarch and Pausanias, but say that they are late.

4 · Source requirement — with what?

Which source must I use — and must I also use other relevant sources? A printed source must be used, not just mentioned.

Then the marks

They set the time (about 1.8 minutes a mark) and the depth: 3 marks is a few precise sentences; 10 marks is a thesis and three or four paragraphs.

A directive verb is the instruction word in an exam question, such as explain.

4 · Taking questions apart · I do — when a source is printed with the question

Use the source — don’t summarise it

  1. 1Name it and its typeFor example: Source L, a poem by Tyrtaeus, probably a Spartan, mid-7th century BC.
  2. 2Quote a specific detailA few words, built into your own sentence.
  3. 3Explain what it showsAbout the question asked — not about the source in general.
  4. 4Test it against other sourcesDoes another writer, or an object, agree?
  5. 5Weigh it where it mattersTie its value and limits to who wrote it, when and why — never a generic the source is biased.
An interpretation instead?

For a historian’s view (2025 Question 11(c), Jean Ducat), identify the historian’s argument, then agree, qualify or challenge it.

HSC-style practice · the source printed with the question

Source A

Source A · Aristotle on the common meals

Also unsatisfactory are the arrangements first established about the commons meals, called the phiditia. For it ought to be the case that funds come from public revenue, just as in Crete: but with the Lakedaimonians everybody has to contribute, even those who are very poor and unable to afford this expense, the result being the reverse of the lawgiver’s intention. For he wishes the arrangement of the common meals to be a democratic feature, but it becomes utterly undemocratic by being arranged in this way. For it is not easy for the very poor to participate, but the ancient mark of citizenship for them is that anyone who cannot pay this tax cannot belong.

Aristotle, Politics 2.9, 1271a26–37 · a philosopher (384–322 BC) based in Athens, writing in the 330s–320s BC · trans. in Cooley D62

Before you use it: who, when, why?

Who and when

Aristotle, a philosopher, writing more than thirty years after Leuctra. His students described the constitutions of many Greek states, Sparta’s among them.

Why

He is criticising: making every member pay produces “the reverse of the lawgiver’s intention”. Cooley: his criticism is “informed by Sparta’s decline in the fourth century”.

What it shows

Paying your share was “the ancient mark of citizenship”: the messes decided who counted as a full citizen.

HSC-style practice · I do — deconstruct the question

What the question is really asking

Assess

NESA: “Make a judgement of value, quality, outcomes, results or size.” How important were the messes — central, important but limited, or overrated? Say it in the first sentence and keep it to the last.

The importance of the syssitia

What the messes did for Spartan society — citizenship, community, the army, equality — not a description of the meal. Terms: syssitia (phiditia), Spartiates, homoioi.

In Spartan society in this period

To 371 BC. Xenophon wrote probably in the late 390s or 380s BC; Source A comes after Leuctra, and Plutarch about AD 100. Use them all — but say when each wrote.

Source A and other relevant sources

Source A in more than one paragraph, plus other named evidence: Xenophon, Plutarch and Cooley’s introduction to the common meals.

10 marks

About 18 minutes · a thesis that answers the question · three or four paragraphs, each ending on importance · about 500–600 words.

HSC-style practice · We do — joint construction

A thesis answers the question

Build it together
  1. Claim — how important were the syssitia?
  2. Because — the two or three biggest reasons
  3. But — one limit or tension in the evidence

The syssitia were … because … but …

Not a thesis — a roadmap

This essay will discuss the syssitia, their role in citizenship and the army, and what Aristotle says about them.

It lists topics and makes no judgement, so the marker learns nothing about your answer.

A thesis · written for this lesson

The syssitia were central to Spartan society: membership marked out the full citizen and bound citizens into a community that probably also fought together, but because every member had to pay his monthly share, the institution that held the citizen body together may also have helped to shrink it.

Claim (central) · because (citizenship; community and army) · but (the cost). The rest of the answer proves it.

Practice and review · 1 of 2 · the chapter’s Quick Check

Quick Check: Section II

  1. How many minutes should you spend on a 7-mark part? About 13 minutes: 7 × 1.8 ≈ 12.6, as in the 2021 time plan.
  2. Name the four parts of question deconstruction. The directive verb, the key terms, the scope and the source requirement — then let the marks set the time and the depth.
  3. What is the difference between outline and explain? Outline: “Sketch in general terms; indicate the main features of.” Explain: “Relate cause and effect” — it must show why and/or how, not just what.
  4. Why must Source K (HSC 2019) be used with care? It is Athenaeus (who flourished c. AD 200) quoting Demetrius of Scepsis (born c. 214 BC): late evidence, and Demetrius’ own reading of the Karneia as a representation of military discipline. Use it and weigh it (Lesson 21).
