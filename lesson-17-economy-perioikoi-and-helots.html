Recall · 2 minutes · cold call — Lessons 7 and 8 in one slide

Two groups you already know

The perioikoi (Lesson 7)
  • ‘those who live around (us)’, in at least 50 towns, almost all in Laconia
  • personally free, with their own land — but no citizen rights at Sparta
  • fought as hoplites alongside the Spartiates
The helots (Lesson 8)
  • unfree Greeks, native to Laconia and Messenia
  • tied to the land, with their own families
  • most worked the land of individual Spartiates

Today’s question is different: not who they were, but what work each group did in the Lacedaemonian economy — and how we know.

1 · The helots: food · I do — follow the barley

Who fed the Spartiates?

Source · Plutarch, on the common messes

[2] They would meet in groups of about fifteen, give or take a few, and each mess member would contribute every month a bushel of barley-meal, eight gallons of wine, five pounds of cheese, and five half-pounds of figs, plus a very small sum of money for “extras”.

Plutarch, Life of Lykourgos 12.2 · written c. AD 100 · trans. B. Wilson · Cooley D63

Where did a Spartiate’s barley, wine, cheese and figs come from?

Helots farm the estatemost of the farm work→ Rent in kindto the master→ Household + mess duesevery month→ He stays a citizenno dues, no rights
Cooley, on the helots’ rent

Helots supplied their master with “a ‘rent’ in kind, which the Spartiate would use to maintain his own household and to meet his contributions to the syssitia”.

Why it mattered

Cooley calls the mess contribution “a kind of property qualification, since men who were not able to meet these contributions would lose their full citizen rights” (Lesson 7). Helot labour paid for citizenship itself.

The big picture

The helots “did most of the agricultural work (in all ancient Greek states agriculture was the basis of economic activity), which gave the Spartiates the resources and free time to maintain their unique society” (Cooley).

1 · The helots: food · We do — two sources, two arrangements

Half the harvest — or a fixed rent?

Source · Tyrtaeus, on the conquered Messenians

Like asses distressed by great burdens,
Bringing to their masters from grievous necessity
Half of all the produce the ploughland bears.

Tyrtaios, fragments 6 and 7 (part, quoted by Pausanias) · mid-7th century BC · probably a Spartan poet · trans. K. Hughes · Cooley A6

Source · Plutarch

The helots worked the land for them, paying a rent established in advance. Charging a higher rent was forbidden, so that the helots might make some profit and be happy to serve, and the masters would not be greedy for more.

Plutarch, Lakonian Customs 41 (Moralia 239E) · written c. AD 100 · trans. in Cooley D36

In a bad year, who loses under each?

A half share: master and helot both lose. A fixed rent: the helot must still pay, so he carries the risk.

In a good year, who gains?

Under a fixed rent the helot keeps the extra — Plutarch’s own reason: “so that the helots might make some profit”.

Which was the classical system?

We cannot be sure. Tyrtaeus describes the conquered Messenians in the 7th century BC; Plutarch writes centuries later. They may describe different times or places (Lesson 8).

Careful: the first two answers are our reasoning about how each arrangement works, not something an ancient writer says. Label reasoning like this as your own inference.

1 · The helots: food · We do — evidence from the fields If time

Where helots lived and worked: the archaeology

Messenia: big villages

Intensive survey found “larger village communities; these evidently housed the helots who worked the land for the Spartans” (W. G. Cavanagh, in Cooley).

A farm building

“A large building excavated at Kopanaki in Messenia contained storage jars and loom weights and other finds indicating agricultural activity.” (Cavanagh)

Not all the same

In Laconia the evidence “seems to point at communities around a large estate”, and “In some areas helots may have lived in scattered farmsteads.” (Cooley)

Running the farms

Most helots “were probably quite independent in organising their activities”, and “It is possible that helots could have private possessions (de facto, if not de iure)” (Cooley).

What can survey show — and what can it not?

Survey shows where people lived and worked; excavation (Kopanaki) shows what they stored. Neither shows who owned the land, or what rent was paid.

2 · The helots: service · I do — work beyond the fields

Service as well as food

Source · Herodotus, on the Greek line at Plataea, 479 BC

[28.2] Ten thousand Lakedaimonians held the right wing: of them, five thousand were Spartiates who were guarded by thirty-five thousand light-armed helots – seven serving each man. … [29.1] All these troops, except the seven men accompanying each Spartiate, were hoplites …

Herodotus, Histories 9.28.2, 9.29.1 (part) · written c. 450s–420s BC · trans. B. Wilson · Cooley E84

Source · Thucydides, on Sphacteria, 425 BC

[5] … the Lakedaimonians had made it known that anyone wishing to take across to the island flour, wine, cheese or any other food that would help those besieged, would be given a large amount of money, and any helots doing so were promised their freedom.

Thucydides, History of the Peloponnesian War 4.26.5 · an Athenian, c. 455–400 BC · trans. B. Wilson · Cooley E92

  1. 1Household servantsWhere a Spartiate family lived on an estate, helots “were probably also employed as household servants” (Cooley).
  2. 2Attendants in war35,000 light-armed helots at Plataea, “seven serving each man” (E84).
  3. 3Supply in warHelots carried food to the Lacedaemonians trapped on Sphacteria — for the promise of freedom (E92).
  4. 4Plunder — and tradeAfter Plataea, “The helots stole many items which they sold to the Aiginetans” (Herodotus 9.80, Cooley E61).
  5. 5SoldiersIn 370 BC, offered freedom: “More than six thousand helots, it is said, enrolled themselves” (Xenophon, Hell. 6.5.29, Cooley E69).

2 · The helots: service · We do — a philosopher’s view

What helot labour bought

Source · Aristotle

That it necessary for a state that is to be well governed to provide leisure from drudgery is a matter of common agreement: but how this is to be provided is not as easy to ascertain. … It seems also, besides anything else, that it is very hard work to keep watch on them and in determining how to deal with them.

Aristotle, Politics 2.9, 1269a34–1269b12 (part), on the helots · written 330s–320s BC · trans. in Cooley D27

The Spartiate way of life

Cooley: it offered “landed property, leisure (no need to work for a living)” — and “Spartiates were not allowed to engage in any form of trade”.

Whose leisure — and whose drudgery?

The Spartiates’ leisure; the helots’ drudgery. Aristotle uses the helots as his example of how hard such a system is to manage.

What did the system cost?

Watching the helots was “very hard work”. Economic role and control were two sides of one system (Lesson 12).

Aristotle wrote after Sparta had lost Messenia (370/69 BC), as a critic looking for causes of its decline.

3 · The perioikoi · I do — Cooley’s note, hedge by hedge

The perioikoi: goods, trade and soldiers

Free to work

“Perioikoi also owned their own land and were free to engage in any economic activity.” Some were wealthy: Cooley reads Xenophon’s “better sort of perioikoi” (E57) that way.

Harbours and trade

All the important harbours, “especially Gytheion, which served as chief naval base, were perioikic communities, and we can assume that perioikoi were involved in overseas trade and fishery”.

Resources

Laconia’s “iron and lead deposits as well as high quality building stone” were “presumably exploited by the perioikoi”.

Goods and soldiers

Goods “produced locally, for example pottery and of course armour and weapons”. The perioikoi fought as hoplites and were “a first line of defence against invasion by land or sea”.

Cooley: “The Spartiates clearly had to rely on a variety of goods and services provided by the perioikoi, although the mechanism of exchange between the two groups remains unknown.”

Quotations from ‘Perioikoi: a note’, in M. G. L. Cooley (ed.), Sparta (2023), pp. 105–106. Notice his hedges: probably, we can assume, presumably.

3 · The perioikoi · We do — partners or subjects? If time

Perioikic land, wealth and soldiers

Source · Plutarch, on Lycurgus’ division of land

He divided the land of Lakonia into 30,000 allotments, and allocated them to the dwellers-round about, the perioikoi.

Plutarch, Life of Lykourgos 8.3 · written c. AD 100 · trans. B. Wilson · Cooley D57

This belongs to the equal-lots story that Cooley calls a “late tradition” (Lesson 15) — but Cooley’s own view is that the perioikoi “owned their own land”.

Source · Xenophon, on a king’s privileges

… specified tracts of high quality land in many of the territories of the perioikoi shall belong to him, sufficient to sustain the royal household adequately but without excessive wealth.

Xenophon, Constitution of the Lakedaimonians 15.3 · probably late 390s or 380s BC · trans. B. Wilson · Cooley D9

Links with the kings

“The wealthiest perioikoi probably had personal connections with leading Spartans, especially the royal families who owned perioikic land.” (Cooley)

Soldiers

At Plataea only 5,000 of the 10,000 Lacedaemonians were Spartiates (E84); the rest were presumably perioikoi. Later, “during the Peloponnesian War, they were incorporated within the Spartiate contingents” (Cooley).

Partners or subjects?

Both: free landowners, probably traders, and fellow hoplites — yet with no citizen rights at Sparta and no foreign policy of their own.

NESA’s 2024 marking guidelines (Question 11(d), “answers could include”): “There is limited evidence for the role of perioikoi in Spartan society, with few textual references.”

4 · Who made the weapons? · We do — read the source for what it does say

The iron-works Cinadon showed, c. 399 BC

Source · Xenophon on the conspiracy of Cinadon

[7] When the ephors asked, “Where did they say they would get the weapons from?”, the informer claimed that Kinadon said, “Those of us in the army obviously have our own weapons,” and that as for the masses, he led him to iron-works and showed him many knives, many swords, many skewers, many axes and hatchets, many sickles. And he said that Kinadon said that all those things that men use to work on the land, on timber, and in quarries, were similarly weapons, and that most other trades had tools that would serve as weapons, especially against unarmed opponents.

Xenophon, Hellenika 3.3.7 · Xenophon lived c. 430–c. 350 BC · trans. in Cooley E53a

What does the source show?

Iron-works stocked with weapons and tools in c. 399 BC (Xenophon does not say where), and other trades with tools of their own.

What work do the tools point to?

Farming, timber and quarrying — the work of the land, the forests and the stone.

Does it say who worked in the iron-works?

No. Xenophon lists the tools, not the workers. And these are Cinadon’s words, reported by an informer to the ephors (Lesson 7).

So who made Sparta’s weapons?

EvidenceWhat it showsWhat it does not show

The economy as a system

5 · How the parts fitted together · Think–pair–share

What happens to the whole system if one group fails?

c. 464 BC: the helots revolt

After an earthquake, the helots, “Together with the neighbouring perioikoi of Thouria and Aithaia, they seceded to Mount Ithome” (Thucydides 1.101.2, Cooley E62). Sparta had to call on its allies, including Athens.

370/69 BC: Messenia lost

The Messenian helots’ “successful bid for freedom in 370/69 BC reduced Sparta to a middling power of small significance” (Cooley).

370 BC: the system leans on them

With a Theban army in Laconia, the Spartiates offered helots freedom to fight, and “More than six thousand helots, it is said, enrolled themselves” (E69). Some perioikoi urged the Thebans on; others defended Laconia (Lesson 7).

So what? Sparta’s citizens depended for food, service, goods and soldiers on two groups without citizen rights. When the helots of Messenia were lost, Sparta’s power shrank.

A model summary

The helots did most of Sparta’s farming and handed over part of each harvest, half in Tyrtaeus and a fixed rent in Plutarch, which fed their masters’ households and paid the mess contributions on which citizenship depended; they also served in war, and probably in households. The perioikoi, free men with their own towns and land, probably handled the trade, fishing, mining and crafts that Spartiates were barred from, including, Cooley infers, the making of armour and weapons, though how they were paid is unknown. Together the two groups freed the Spartiates for politics and war, so Sparta’s power rested on people without citizen rights, as the revolt of c. 464 BC and the loss of Messenia in 370/69 BC showed.

Judgement, the helots, the perioikoi, the limits

The helots and perioikoi did most of the work that kept Sparta’s economy running, which freed the Spartiates, who were barred from making money, to be full-time citizens and soldiers.1 The helots farmed Spartiate estates and handed over part of each harvest: Tyrtaeus has the conquered Messenians bring their masters ‘Half of all the produce the ploughland bears’, while Plutarch, writing about AD 100, describes ‘a rent established in advance’.2 This rent in kind fed each Spartiate’s household and paid his monthly mess contribution, so helot labour paid for citizenship itself.3 Helots also served in war: at Plataea in 479 BC, Herodotus says, light-armed helots attended the Spartiates, ‘seven serving each man’.4 The perioikoi, free men with their own towns and land, probably did the work Spartiates could not: Cooley suggests they traded overseas and fished from harbour towns such as Gytheion, and presumably exploited Laconia’s iron, lead and building stone.5 Cinadon could show an informer iron-works full of ‘many knives, many swords’, but Xenophon never says who worked there, so perioikic weapon-makers are an inference, not a fact.6 Together, the two groups supplied the food, service, goods and soldiers of a citizen body that did not work for a living, though how the Spartiates paid the perioikoi ‘remains unknown’.7

Why answers drop marks

2–3 marks · a list

The helots were farmers and the perioikoi were traders and craftsmen. Both groups fought in the army. The Spartiates did not have to work.

Accurate, but nothing explains why the roles mattered, and no source is named: “some information”, not an explanation.

4 marks · one group only

The helots farmed the Spartiates’ land and paid a fixed rent, as Plutarch says, which paid each man’s mess contribution, so …

Some real explanation of the helots — but the perioikoi, half the question, are missing.

Trap · certainty the sources don’t give

The perioikoi made all of Sparta’s weapons, as Xenophon proves with Cinadon’s iron-works, and every helot paid exactly half his harvest.

Two hedges lost. Xenophon never says who worked the iron-works; “half” is Tyrtaeus on the conquered Messenians, and Plutarch gives a fixed rent.

Practice and review · 2 of 6 · true, false, justify · written for this lesson

True or false? Justify with a source

  1. 1Tyrtaeus says the conquered Messenians handed over half of their produce. True — “Half of all the produce the ploughland bears” (A6).
  2. 2Plutarch says a master could raise the helots’ rent after a good harvest. False — “Charging a higher rent was forbidden” (D36).
  3. 3Xenophon says who worked in the iron-works Cinadon showed the informer. False — he lists the tools, not the workers (E53a); perioikoi probably did such work (Cooley).
  4. 4Xenophon says Spartiates could work as merchants. False — Lycurgus “refused to allow free citizens to have anything to do with money making” (D53).
  5. 5Cooley says the perioikoi owned their own land. True — “Perioikoi also owned their own land” (‘Perioikoi: a note’).
  6. 6At Plataea every Lacedaemonian on the right wing was a Spartiate. False — 5,000 of the 10,000 were Spartiates (E84); the rest were presumably perioikoi.
  7. 7Cooley says we know how the Spartiates paid the perioikoi for their goods. False — “the mechanism of exchange between the two groups remains unknown”.
  8. 8Survey in Messenia found large villages that housed helots. True, as Cavanagh reads it — villages that “evidently housed the helots who worked the land for the Spartans”; Cooley is more cautious: the evidence “seems to suggest” large villages.

A model paragraph · written for this lesson

Xenophon’s account is useful evidence that weapons and tools were made in Laconia, but only indirect evidence that the perioikoi made them. The informer says Cinadon ‘led him to iron-works and showed him many knives, many swords, many skewers, many axes and hatchets, many sickles’, and claimed that the tools men use to ‘work on the land, on timber, and in quarries’ could serve as weapons. This shows a working iron industry and a range of trades in Laconia around 399 BC, in a society where, Xenophon says elsewhere, Lycurgus ‘refused to allow free citizens to have anything to do with money making’, so the workers were presumably not Spartiates. Yet Xenophon never names them, and the words are a conspirator’s, reported by an informer to the ephors; Cooley can say only that the perioikoi ‘probably handled most of the economic activities that were prohibited to Lakedaimonians’. The passage therefore supports the view that non-citizens supplied Sparta’s weapons, but it leaves the perioikoi’s part as a strong inference rather than a fact.

To what extent did the Spartan economy depend on the perioikoi and helots in this period? (15 marks)

Use evidence from relevant sources. Plan it in four moves:

  1. Thesis in your first sentence — for example: heavily, because Spartiates were barred from money-making — though much of the perioikoi’s part rests on inference.
  2. The helots: rent in kind (Tyrtaeus A6; Plutarch D36) → household and mess dues (Plutarch D63) → citizenship; service in war (Herodotus E84; Thucydides E92); the Messenian survey (Cavanagh).

Practice and review · 6 of 6 · short review · written for this lesson

Review: perioikoi and helots in the economy

  1. What did the helots’ rent in kind pay for? The master’s household and his monthly contribution to his mess — and so his citizenship.
  2. Name the four foods in Plutarch’s list of each man’s monthly mess contribution. Barley-meal, wine, cheese and figs (plus a little money), Plutarch, Lykourgos 12.2 (D63).
  3. Why might a helot prefer a fixed rent in a good year? He kept whatever was left over; Plutarch says the rent was fixed “so that the helots might make some profit”.
  4. Give two kinds of service helots gave in war, with evidence. Any two: attendants at Plataea (E84); carrying food to Sphacteria (E92); collecting plunder (E61); soldiers in 370 BC (E69).
  5. Which three hedges does Cooley use about the perioikoi’s economic work? “probably” (most economic activities), “we can assume” (trade and fishing), “presumably” (iron, lead and stone).
  6. Why is Xenophon’s account of the iron-works not proof that the perioikoi made Sparta’s weapons? It never names the workers, and the words are Cinadon’s, reported second-hand by an informer.
  7. Correct the textbook diagram that says both copper and tin were imported. Tin was imported; copper was possibly local, from deposits near Boia that Cavanagh says “may well have been tapped in antiquity”.
  8. What does Cooley say we do not know about the Spartiates and the perioikoi? How goods passed between them: “the mechanism of exchange between the two groups remains unknown”.
