1 · One system · I do — one chain, thought aloud · 8 minutes

One chain — and it loops back

  1. 1Helots farmed the land and handed over part of the harvestTyrtaeus: “Half of all the produce the ploughland bears” (Cooley A6) L8
  2. 2The harvest paid each Spartiate’s monthly mess contributionbarley-meal, wine, cheese and figs — Plutarch, Lykourgos 12 (Cooley D63) L12
  3. 3Mess membership was the mark of citizenshipAristotle: “anyone who cannot pay this tax cannot belong” (Cooley D62) L12
  4. 4Men who could not pay probably fell to the hypomeionesCooley: “presumably descendants of homoioi who had lost their status” (note on E53b) L7
  5. 5Inheritance and dowries concentrated land in fewer handsAristotle, writing after Leuctra: “almost two-fifths of the whole country is owned by women” (Cooley D54) L14
  6. 6Fewer citizens meant fewer hoplitesSpartiates fell from about 8,000 in 480 BC to an estimated c. 1,500 by 371 BC (Cooley’s table, D29); at Leuctra “four hundred of the seven hundred Spartiates present had fallen” (Xenophon, Cooley E109) L7L28
  7. 7Defeat let Thebes free Messenia and its helots — so much of the land that fed the messes was lostMessene refounded, 370/69 BC (Diodorus, Cooley H71): step 7 cuts into step 1 L28
The loop

The chain ends where it began: losing Messenia’s helots and land broke the first link and weakened every link after it.

Reading the map

Which features have the most links? Which has the fewest?

The army and the economy (five each). Fewest: women and land (one) — but fewest is not least important: Aristotle linked the shortage of citizens to land concentrated in few hands, with almost two-fifths of the country owned by women (D54, D28).

Pull one thread: Messenia is lost in 370/69 BC. Follow the arrows.

Links 1 → 2 → 6 and 7: less helot produce, fewer men able to pay their mess dues, a smaller army — and the helots of Messenia now free (Lesson 28).

Which link is only “probably”? What should your answer do with it?

Link 6: Cooley says the messes “probably formed a basic grouping within the army”. Keep his “probably” in your own writing.

Check for understanding · gesture vote · hold up 1–4 fingers

Which feature best explains both Sparta’s strength and its weakness?

  1. 1The helot system
  2. 2The agoge
  3. 3Citizenship tied to land and the messes
  4. 4The dual kingship
Choice 1, argued

Helot labour freed the citizens (A6) — but where the helots were concerned, security always came first (Thucydides, D39), and losing Messenia broke the system (H71).

Choice 3, argued

Mess dues decided who counted as one of the “Equals” (homoioi), the exclusive citizen body (D62; Cooley, introduction to D52–D59) — but as land concentrated, the citizens fell, by Aristotle’s day, to “not even one thousand” (D28).

There is no single right answer: any choice earns marks if you argue both sides with evidence.

3 · Sparta after Sparta · I do

After Leuctra: the city Plutarch and Pausanias knew

After Leuctra

Sparta survived as a small state. Cooley: the Messenian helots’ “successful bid for freedom in 370/69 BC reduced Sparta to a middling power of small significance” (Lesson 28).

Cooley, on reading the later writers

in order to interpret later authors’ reports of Sparta, we also have to understand the life of the Hellenistic and Roman city

Cooley, introduction to section F (“The Spartan Mirage”), citing Cartledge & Spawforth, Hellenistic and Roman Sparta

Quick Check · section 6

Why must we understand Roman Sparta to read Plutarch and Pausanias? They knew the Sparta of their own day, where old customs had been restored, reinvented or staged — so what they report as ancient may not be classical.

What did that later city do with its famous past?

It restored — or re-invented — it

The Spartans “tried to restore (or re-invent) their ancestral constitution”, and “many new ideas were presented as ancient” (Cooley, citing Kennell and Cartledge & Spawforth). L19L27

It staged it for visitors

The whipping at the altar of Artemis Orthia: Cooley calls it “not classical but designed for sadistic Roman tourists” (note on C94). L10

It honoured its heroes

At the memorial of Leonidas, Pausanias saw that “Every year speeches are made there and there is a contest in which only Spartiates may compete” (3.14.1, Cooley F27). L22

3 · Sparta after Sparta · We do — two sayings about Leonidas

The Spartans helped build the mirage

Source · Pausanias

Before Leuktra, no setback had ever befallen the Lakedaimonians, with the result that they did not admit that they had ever been beaten in an infantry contest: for they say that Leonidas won but had insufficient followers for a wholesale slaughter of the Persians

Pausanias, Description of Greece 1.13.5 · 2nd century AD · trans. in Cooley F8

Even Thermopylae was retold as a kind of victory. Cooley: the Spartans “played a crucial role in creating and maintaining the Spartan mirage”.

Source · Plutarch, a saying of Leonidas

When Xerxes wrote to him, “Hand over your weapons,” he wrote back, “Come and get them.”

Plutarch, Sayings of Lakedaimonians, Leonidas 10 (Moralia 225A–E) · late 1st or early 2nd century AD, nearly six centuries after Thermopylae · trans. in Cooley F31. In Greek, “Come and get them” is molōn labe.

Can we trust it?

Cooley on Plutarch’s collection of sayings: “We cannot know whether any of these are authentic, but together they can serve as a perfect illustration of the main themes of the Spartan ‘mirage’.”

A model summary · written for this lesson

Sparta’s features formed one system: helot labour paid the mess dues that made a man a citizen, education made citizens into soldiers, the messes probably formed the army’s basic units, and kings, ephors and festivals bound war, religion and helot control together. Because each feature rested on the others, a weakness in one — fewer citizens as land concentrated, or the loss of Messenia’s helots — spread through all of them. After Leuctra, later Spartans, Plutarch, painters, film-makers and modern politics kept reinventing Sparta, so its legacy often tells us more about them than about Sparta.

HSC-style synthesis · I do — an annotated model paragraph (thread 1) · written for this lesson

A paragraph that follows one thread

The deepest link in the Spartan system ran from helot labour, through the land, to citizenship itself.1 The helots brought their masters ‘Half of all the produce the ploughland bears’, in the words of Tyrtaeus, a 7th-century poet who was probably a Spartan,2 so the citizens did not need to farm and could give their lives to training, the army and the messes.3 That produce paid each man’s monthly contribution of barley-meal, wine, cheese and figs, which Plutarch lists (Lycurgus 12), and Aristotle shows what hung on it: ‘anyone who cannot pay this tax cannot belong’.4 Land therefore decided citizenship, and citizenship decided the size of the army.5 Aristotle wrote after Leuctra and Plutarch around AD 100, so both look back on the system; but Xenophon, a contemporary, records that only seven hundred Spartiates were present at Leuctra, which suggests the citizen body had already shrunk.6 The link that freed Spartiates to be citizen soldiers also tied their numbers to the land, so when Messenia and its helots were lost in 370/69 BC, the system lost much of its base.7

Practice and review · 2 of 6 · true, false, justify · written for this lesson

True or false? Justify with a source

  1. 1Cooley says the Spartan education was a precondition for becoming a citizen. True — “This special Spartan education was a precondition for becoming a citizen” (introduction to D73–D81).
  2. 2Herodotus says the Spartan kings held two priesthoods. True — of “Zeus of Lakedaimon and Zeus, Lord of Heaven” (Herodotus 6.56, D5).
  3. 3The sources prove that each mess was a unit of the army. False — Cooley says only that messes “probably formed a basic grouping within the army”.
  4. 4Aristotle, writing before Leuctra, warned that Spartan land was falling into the hands of a few. False — he wrote in the 330s–320s BC, after Leuctra, about his own day (D54).
  5. 5Herodotus is the earliest surviving source for Leonidas’ reply “Come and get them”. False — it is in Plutarch’s Sayings, c. AD 100 (F31). Herodotus’ famous line is Dienekes’ “fighting in the shade” (7.226, F20). Cooley’s Thermopylae introduction loosely lists ‘come and get them’ among Herodotus’ sayings, but no passage of Herodotus contains it.
  6. 6Cooley thinks the sayings Plutarch collected are authentic. False — “We cannot know whether any of these are authentic” (introduction to section F).
  7. 7Pausanias says that before Leuctra the Spartans would not admit they had ever been beaten in an infantry battle. True — “they did not admit that they had ever been beaten in an infantry contest” (Pausanias 1.13.5, F8).
  8. 8Helots went to war with the Spartan army. True — at Plataea, “five thousand were Spartiates who were guarded by thirty-five thousand light-armed helots” (Herodotus 9.28, E84).

How far was the helot system the feature on which all the others depended?

JudgeOpen with a qualified judgement: how far, and why not entirely? EvidenceUse at least two links from the map, each with a named source and its date. ResolveClose with your refined judgement — not a repeat of your first sentence.
A model paragraph · written for this lesson

The helot system was the base on which the other features rested, but it did not stand alone, because it needed an army and a citizen body to keep it in place. Helots handed over ‘Half of all the produce the ploughland bears’ (Tyrtaeus), and that produce paid the mess dues on which citizenship depended, since anyone who could not pay ‘cannot belong’ (Aristotle). Free of farm work, citizens could give their lives to training and war. Yet the dependence ran both ways: Thucydides says that where the helots were concerned, considerations of security came first, and Aristotle, as reported by Plutarch, says the ephors declared war on them on taking office. Much of this evidence is late or written after Leuctra, but the loss of Messenia in 370/69 BC bears it out: when the helots of Messenia were freed, the system that rested on them could not be rebuilt. The helots were the foundation of the system, but the army, the messes and the ephors were what held them in place.

Practice and review · 6 of 6 · chapter review, sections 5–6

Chapter review: one system, and its legacy

  1. Explain one chain that links helots, the syssitia and citizenship. Helots handed over “Half of all the produce the ploughland bears” (Tyrtaeus, A6); this paid each man’s monthly mess contribution (Plutarch, D63); and a man who could not pay lost his place, since “anyone who cannot pay this tax cannot belong” (Aristotle, D62). Citizenship therefore rested on helot labour.
  2. How has Sparta been reinvented in modern times? Use one image as evidence. As a model of heroic resistance and a slogan. The 2020 flag turns Leonidas’ “molōn labe”, first found in Plutarch c. AD 100, into a modern political slogan; the 1962 poster sells Thermopylae as a film. Both show their makers’ concerns, and leave out the helots and perioikoi who probably died there too (Cooley).
  3. Why does the education system belong on the interrelation map? Cooley: education was “a precondition for becoming a citizen”, its aim was election to a mess, and it was followed by military training until 30 — so it linked citizenship, the messes and the army.
  4. What did the kings link together? War, religion and government: the king led the army “on all expeditions” and swore a monthly oath with the ephors (Xenophon, D9), and held “two priesthoods” (Herodotus, D5).
  5. Why must Plutarch and Pausanias be used with care for classical Sparta? They wrote in the Roman period and knew a Sparta that had restored, or re-invented, its customs and staged some for visitors (Cooley; the Orthia whipping, note on C94).
  6. Explain how one weakness spread through the Spartan system. As land concentrated through inheritance and dowries (D54), fewer men could pay their mess dues (D62), so citizen numbers fell: from about 8,000 in 480 BC to an estimated c. 1,500 by 371 BC (Cooley’s table, D29), and by Aristotle’s day “not even one thousand” (D28). At Leuctra “four hundred of the seven hundred Spartiates present had fallen” (Xenophon, E109), and the defeat cost Sparta Messenia and its helots (H71).
