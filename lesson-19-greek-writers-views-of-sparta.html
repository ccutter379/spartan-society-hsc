1 · Xenophon: admirer and critic · I do

Xenophon: the best-placed outsider

  1. 1An Athenian, c. 430–c. 350 BCFrom a wealthy family — a soldier and a writer.
  2. 2He fought for a Spartan kingHe served the Spartan king Agesilaus, including at Coronea (394 BC), where Athens was on the other side. Cooley links this to his exile from Athens.
  3. 3Sparta looked after himThe Spartans gave him an estate near Olympia. Cooley: he “may have lived in Sparta and even had his sons participate in the Spartan education system”.
  4. 4Two works for SpartaThe Constitution of the Lacedaemonians, “probably written in the late 390’s or 380’s BC”, and the Hellenika, a history of 411–362 BC.
Close — and grateful

Cooley: “Certainly he had access to first hand information about Sparta and reason to be grateful to Agesilaos and Sparta.”

A laconophile

Cooley calls the Constitution “a kind of political pamphlet, defending Sparta against its critics”. Except, that is, for one chapter.

1 · Xenophon: admirer and critic · We do — two extracts, one writer

Xenophon on Sparta, admired and fallen

Source · Xenophon · Extract A

It is clear that these laws are of great antiquity, since Lykourgos is said to have lived in the days of the Heraklids, the sons of Herakles. But though they are so very old, even today they still seem very odd to the rest of Greece. What makes it even more extraordinary is that everyone admires these arrangements, but no state wants to imitate them.

Extract B

[1] If someone were to ask me whether I think that Lykourgos’ laws remain unchanged to this day, I have to confess in all honesty that I could not say so with any confidence. … [3] I know, too, that in former days they were frightened to be caught in possession of gold; whereas nowadays some of them are even happy to make a display of their wealth. … [8] But none of these criticisms is at all surprising, since it is all too clear that modern Lakedaimonians obey the laws of neither the gods nor Lykourgos.

Xenophon, Constitution of the Lakedaimonians 10.8 (Extract A) and 14.1, 3, 8 (Extract B) · the work probably late 390s or 380s BC; the date of chapter 14 is debated · trans. B. Wilson · Cooley F1, E70

What picture does Extract A give?

Laws so old they go back to the Heraclids, unique in Greece, and admired by “everyone”.

And Extract B?

Spartans of his own day who flaunt their wealth and no longer keep Lycurgus’ laws.

Who does he blame?

The Spartans, not the laws. Even his criticism keeps faith with Lycurgus.

1 · Xenophon: admirer and critic · We do — OVPRL, then the verdict

What chapter 14 changes

AskModel answer
OHe lived under Spartan protection. How does that help and hinder him?Helps: he saw Sparta at first hand. Hinders: he owed Sparta his home, so he wrote as a friend.
VWhat two pictures of Sparta do the extracts give?An ancient, admired system — and Spartans of his day who had abandoned it.
PWhy might an admirer include a chapter of criticism?To defend the laws by blaming the people who broke them.
RWhich extract is more likely to be shaped by his feelings?Both: A by admiration, B by disappointment. But B is the more persuasive, because it cuts against his own sympathies.
LHe contrasts “former days” with “nowadays”. Why is it hard to know when his ideal Sparta existed?He never dates the good days, and his lawgiver lived “in the days of the Heraklids”. The perfect Sparta may be an ideal, not a period (section 4).
When was chapter 14 written?

Debated. Cooley heads it “c. 380 BC”, but his note says it “must surely represent Xenophon’s disillusionment with Sparta after the catastrophic defeat at Leuktra in 371 BC”. Either way, it is “generally considered later” than the rest of the work, though Cooley adds “it is not clear whether it was added after the rest”.

The rest of the book

Cooley: “a consistently admiring standpoint, with the solitary exception of this ‘notoriously problematic chapter’”.

A gift to historians

Sparta’s best-placed admirer admits the laws were no longer obeyed. That suggests the perfect Sparta of the past was partly an ideal.

1 · Xenophon: admirer and critic · the Hellenika If time

What a contemporary leaves out

Leuctra, told from one side

His Hellenika is our main contemporary account of Leuctra. But Cooley: “Xenophon, though contemporary, gives his account entirely from the Spartan side: he does not once in his whole account mention Epaminondas, architect of the Theban victory”.

And afterwards

Thebes refounded Messene in 369 BC. Cooley: “No direct mention of this major event is recorded by Xenophon who presumably could not bear to narrate this disaster for Spartans”.

But he is not simply a flatterer. What does this show?

On Leuctra he is blunt about the Spartan horsemen: “at that time their cavalry were an absolute disgrace” (Hellenika 6.4.10–11, Cooley E107).

Reliable for what?

The Spartan side of events in his own lifetime. Not the Theban side: for that Cooley points to Plutarch’s Life of Pelopidas, “the best account from the Theban perspective”.

2 · Aristotle: ‘a single blow’ · I do

Aristotle: the critic with hindsight

  1. 1384–322 BCFrom Stagira in Chalcidice, northern Greece; he worked in Athens. A boy at the time of Leuctra.
  2. 2A researcher of constitutionsHis students “researched and described 158 constitutions, among them that of Sparta”. That study is lost, but it “was used extensively by later authors, including Plutarch”.
  3. 3The Politics, Book 2 · 330s–320s BCHe tests Sparta’s laws “in comparison with the best arrangement” (Politics 1269a, Cooley D47) — so he looks for faults.
Cooley

“He offers valuable criticism of the Spartan system, informed by Sparta’s decline in the fourth century.”

Hodkinson, quoted in Cooley

In the Politics, Aristotle’s “recognition of certain laudable features is outweighed by his severe criticism of a number of fundamental flaws”.

2 · Aristotle: ‘a single blow’ · We do — reading a source with OVPRL

Aristotle on why Sparta fell

Source · Aristotle

As a result, although the land could support one thousand five hundred cavalry and thirty thousand hoplites, the total number was not even one thousand. What has actually taken place has made it clear that their arrangements in this were at fault, given that the state did not withstand a single blow but was destroyed through its oliganthropia.

Aristotle, Politics 2.9, from 1270a29 · written 330s–320s BC · trans. in Cooley D28

The single blow is Leuctra. Oliganthropia: a shortage of citizen manpower. “As a result” follows his complaint that “the land has fallen into the hands of a small minority” (Cooley D54).

AskModel answer
OWhat had recently happened to Sparta when he wrote?Defeat at Leuctra (371 BC) and the loss of Messenia (370/69 BC); he wrote decades later.
VWhat does he say happened to the number of citizens?Land enough for 30,000 hoplites and 1,500 cavalry, yet “not even one thousand”.
PHe is diagnosing a failure. How might that affect what he looks for?He looks for the faults that caused it — in the laws themselves.
RHow might hindsight shape his judgement?Knowing the ending, he reads the collapse back into the whole system.
LDoes this tell us how Sparta worked in the 6th or 5th century BC?Not directly: it describes the 4th-century crisis. He can make 4th-century problems look permanent.

2 · Aristotle: ‘a single blow’ · I do — two writers, two diagnoses

Why did Sparta fail? Two diagnoses

Xenophon: the Spartans broke the laws

“it is all too clear that modern Lakedaimonians obey the laws of neither the gods nor Lykourgos” (Cooley E70). The laws were good; the people failed them.

v.
Aristotle: the laws were at fault

“their arrangements in this were at fault” (Cooley D28): land “has fallen into the hands of a small minority, and this through bad regulation of the laws” (D54).

Cooley weighs them

“Ancient authors generally prefer the idea that Sparta’s decline was due to a neglect of her ancient customs (e.g. E70).” But “one might suggest that Sparta could not actually halt the decline precisely because she maintained the old laws for too long”.

Writers disagree

Aristotle blames the women too: “For they live wantonly in absolutely every way, and luxuriously” (D69). Plutarch replies that “Aristotle is wrong” to say Lycurgus gave up trying to discipline them (D70). Check every writer against another.

3 · Pausanias and Plutarch · We do — the traveller

Pausanias: what he saw, and what he was told

Source · Pausanias, on the altar of Artemis Orthia

There was an oracle that the altar should be bloodied with human blood: this used to be someone chosen by lot, but Lykourgos changed this to a whipping of ephebes, so that the altar could get its fill of human blood in this way: the priestess stands by them, holding the wooden statue of the goddess. It is small and therefore normally light, but if the floggers hold back their strokes because of an ephebe’s good looks or noble birth, then the statue becomes too heavy for the woman to carry, and she blames the floggers and says that it is their fault that she is struggling.

Pausanias, Description of Greece 3.16.10–11 · from Asia Minor, writing in the 2nd century AD · trans. in Cooley C93

Who was Pausanias?

He wrote a Description of Greece in ten books, “based on his own visits”. Cooley: he “was particularly interested in ancient monuments, cults and traditions, and offers many useful details on these, often confirmed by archaeology”.

Which part is a story he was told?

The origin: an oracle, human sacrifice, Lycurgus’ change. He repeats a local story — he does not say where it came from.

Which part describes the ritual of his own day?

The priestess and the statue, in the present tense: the ritual as it was done in Roman Sparta. He does not say he watched it.

Reliable for what?

Good evidence for the ritual as it was performed in his own Roman day, and poor evidence for how it began. Cooley: the flogging contest “was not classical but designed for sadistic Roman tourists”.

3 · Pausanias and Plutarch · We do — the moralist

Plutarch begins his Life of Lycurgus — with a warning

Source · Plutarch

[1] There is a general lack of agreement about the life of Lykourgos, the lawgiver, both in its broad perspective and its particulars, since accounts vary about his origins, travels, and death. Above all, the record of his legal and constitutional reforms is inconsistent, the history of the times in which he lived being, perhaps, the most widely disputed subject of all.

Plutarch, Life of Lykourgos 1.1 · written c. AD 100 · from Chaeronea in Boeotia · trans. B. Wilson · Cooley F2

His strength

A “diligent researcher: often preserving information that is otherwise lost”. In the Lycurgus he cites over fifty earlier works (Cooley).

AskModel answer
OHow far was Plutarch in time from the lawgiver he describes?Many centuries: he wrote c. AD 100, and even Herodotus placed Lycurgus in the distant past (Lesson 3).
VWhat does this opening reveal about the evidence in front of him?The sources disagreed on everything: origins, travels, death, reforms and dates.
PHe wrote a full biography anyway. What does that suggest about his aims?Moral, not historical. Cooley: “his main aim was to draw characters which could be instructive as moral examples”.
RIs a writer who admits doubt more trustworthy?More honest about his evidence — but he still chose and shaped the stories he told.
LWhat might Plutarch take from his own Roman-era world?Later customs. Cooley: “much of his material also dates from after Sparta’s decline in the fourth century”.

3 · Pausanias and Plutarch · watching a moralist at work If time

Plutarch at work: the krypteia

Source · Plutarch

[28.1] … The so-called krypteia, if this was indeed one of Lykourgos’ institutions, as Aristotle records, may be the reason for Plato’s having reaching the same conclusion about Lykourgos and his constitution. …

[6] My own view is that such savage ill treatment by the Lakedaimonians was in fact a later development, consequent upon the great earthquake … I certainly could never attribute to Lykourgos a measure as abominable as the krypteia, something so utterly alien to his general record of gentleness of character and concern for justice.

Plutarch, Life of Lykourgos 28.1, 6 · written c. AD 100 · trans. B. Wilson · Cooley D44

What does Plutarch preserve?

A lost work: Aristotle’s account that Lycurgus founded the krypteia.

Why does he reject it?

Not on evidence, but on character: it does not fit his good lawgiver. The moral aim shapes the history.

Reliable for what?

What earlier writers said, when he names them. Not for Lycurgus’ character, which is his own creation.

3 · Pausanias and Plutarch · Check for understanding · cold call

Quick check: the later witnesses

  1. Why is Xenophon called a laconophile? He admired Sparta: his Constitution praises the laws of Lycurgus and defends Sparta against its critics. He fought for Agesilaus and lived on an estate the Spartans gave him.
  2. What does Xenophon admit in chapter 14 of his Constitution? That Lycurgus’ laws were no longer kept: Spartans of his day flaunted wealth, sought power abroad, and “obey the laws of neither the gods nor Lykourgos”.
  3. What does Aristotle blame for Sparta’s collapse? Oliganthropia, a shortage of citizens: the land could support 30,000 hoplites, but there were “not even one thousand”, because land had fallen into few hands. So the state “did not withstand a single blow” — Leuctra.

Then: which of today’s four writers would you trust least for life in Sparta before 371 BC — and why?

4 · The mirage, deepened · I do — layers of tradition, oldest at the bottom

When did the Spartan mirage begin?

  1. 7th century BCTyrtaeus, probably a Spartan, praises the man who “completely forgets shameful flight” — Sparta’s own ideal.
  2. 5th century BCHerodotus asks what made Sparta strong and imagines Demaratus’ speech; the Athenian Critias (c. 460–403 BC) writes in admiration of Sparta. Cooley: it seems to start here
  3. early 4th century BCXenophon’s Lycurgus becomes, in Cooley’s words, “a wise and prescient figure who had an ingenious solution for almost any problem”.
  4. after 371 BCSparta falls. Aristotle criticises; later writers describe a Sparta that no longer existed. NESA 2024: it starts here
  5. Hellenistic and RomanSpartans “tried to restore (or re-invent) their ancestral constitution”; Plutarch collects Spartan sayings; Pausanias tours the Roman town.
  6. 1933 → todayOllier names the mirage (1933); films such as The 300 Spartans (1962) and 300 (2006) keep reinventing it.
NESA’s 2024 sample answer

“After the collapse of Sparta, Greek writers viewed Spartan society positively, thus creating the Spartan mirage.”

Cooley says

“Many literary texts from the fifth century BC onwards reflect an idealisation and distortion of traditions about Spartan society”. Critias “seems to have started the Spartan ‘mirage’”.

So

The mirage was already forming in the fifth century, with the Spartans’ help, and kept growing after Sparta fell. If you use NESA’s point, add that it began earlier.

4 · The mirage, deepened · why it lasted If time

Why was the image of Sparta so attractive?

  1. 1PowerSparta “dominated interstate politics in mainland Greece throughout the Archaic and Classical period”.
  2. 2A puzzle“the question about Sparta’s secret of success was already a matter of discussion in the fifth century”.
  3. 3A model for those who feared changeSparta “seemed impervious to change and conspicuously old-fashioned”, so “all those who hankered for the good old times and feared decadence could look to Sparta”.
  4. 4Mystique“The relative difficulty of visiting Sparta and getting to know the Spartans themselves at first hand would have contributed to their mystique.”
  5. 5Good stories“Many traditions about Spartans were simply good stories to pass on”: Thermopylae, and short, sharp Laconic sayings.
And the Spartans knew it

Cooley: “The Spartans themselves clearly understood that their reputation abroad was an asset”.

4 · The mirage, deepened · We do — think, pair, share

Does Xenophon’s criticism take him outside the mirage?

Think, then pair

Chapter 14 attacks the Spartans of his own day. Does that make Xenophon a witness against the mirage?

Look at what he takes for granted

A perfect Sparta in “former days”. Cooley: “Many of our sources, especially Xenophon (Lak. Pol.) describe an ancient system, but also suggest that the ideal Spartan lifestyle was a thing of the past.”

Cooley’s question

“If this could be said as early as in the early fourth century, perhaps the Spartans never really fully complied with the strict standards we hear about” — an ideal system of “‘the good old days’ (that never were)”.

Admirers and critics share one picture

“Outsiders’ views of Sparta cannot avoid these stereotypes: favourable comments as well as criticism (e.g. modern comparisons with totalitarian states) are based on the same familiar elements of the legend.”

So: criticising Sparta is not the same as escaping the mirage. Ask what picture of Sparta a writer takes for granted.

A model summary

Xenophon admired Sparta’s laws but admitted that the Spartans of his day no longer kept them; Aristotle, writing after Leuctra, blamed the laws themselves for a shortage of citizens; Pausanias described the Roman town he visited; and Plutarch preserved lost writers while admitting his doubts and shaping Lycurgus as a moral example. The idealised picture of Sparta was already forming in the fifth century BC with admirers such as Critias, grew with Xenophon and was shaped by the Spartans themselves too, and later writers kept adding to it. So historians ask what each writer is reliable for, judging his date, origin, purpose and access to Sparta, rather than trusting or rejecting him as a whole.

NESA’s sample answer — and one claim Cooley contradicts

Greek writers often wrote about the militaristic nature of Spartan society as promoted in Tyrtaeus’ military songs.1 Aristotle and Plato viewed Sparta as having a mixed constitution, with Plato admiring aspects of Spartan institutions and customs.2 Athenian writers, such as Thucydides, viewed Spartan society as unchanging.3 After the collapse of Sparta, Greek writers viewed Spartan society positively, thus creating the Spartan mirage.4

Sample answer from the NESA 2024 HSC Ancient History Marking Guidelines · 62 words

Marker’s margin

1A named writer — but who was he?Tyrtaeus is named, with what his songs promoted. But Cooley calls him “probably Spartan”: this is Sparta’s own ideal, not an outsider’s view. “Greek writers often” is the general phrasing the markers asked students to replace with specific writers.
2Two more writers and a precise term“Mixed constitution” is the right concept. But Aristotle mostly criticised: Hodkinson (in Cooley) says his “recognition of certain laudable features is outweighed by his severe criticism”. Plato’s Laws treats Sparta as “a broadly well-governed polis” but has “explicit criticism of several fundamental flaws”.
3A view with a source behind itThucydides says the Spartans had kept the same constitution for “four hundred years and more” (1.18.1, Cooley F4). Cooley says the system “did evolve”, so this is a view, not a fact — and one writer is not all “Athenian writers”.
4Cooley says: it began earlierThe idealisation runs “from the fifth century BC onwards”; Critias “seems to have started” it, and the Spartans “played a crucial role”. After the collapse, Aristotle was a critic, not an admirer.
NESA’s sample. Four views from four named writers in 62 words, ending on a key concept. Sentence 4’s timing is contradicted by Cooley; sentences 1–3 would be stronger with a reason and a detail each.

Even sharper: for each writer, add who he was and when he wrote, and one precise detail. That is what turns a list of views into an informed explanation.

HSC 2024 Q11(b) · Check your answer — a model written for this lesson

Five writers, each with a view and a reason

Greek writers viewed Sparta through their own purposes, so their views range from admiration to criticism.1 Tyrtaeus, one of only two probably Spartan poets whose work survives, praised the citizen who dies standing firm in battle — ‘Never does his good fame perish nor his name’ — presenting Sparta as a community bound by courage.2 Herodotus, writing c. 450s–420s BC, has the exiled king Demaratus tell Xerxes that the Spartans ‘have a master above them – the law’, an outsider’s admiration for Spartan discipline,3 while the Athenian Thucydides admitted that ‘The secret nature of their political arrangements’ hid even the numbers of their army.4 Xenophon, who lived under Spartan protection, praised Lycurgus’ training, designed to make boys ‘better soldiers overall’, but Aristotle, writing after Leuctra, criticised a state where ‘the land has fallen into the hands of a small minority’.5 Such admiring views, which Cooley traces from the fifth century BC onwards and which the Spartans themselves encouraged, built the idealised ‘Spartan mirage’.6

Practice and review · 2 of 5 · true, false, justify · written for this lesson

True or false? Justify with a source

  1. 1Xenophon praises Sparta in every chapter of his Constitution of the Lacedaemonians. False — chapter 14 criticises Spartans who “obey the laws of neither the gods nor Lykourgos” (Cooley E70). Cooley: the work is admiring “with the solitary exception” of that chapter.
  2. 2Xenophon’s account of Leuctra never names Epaminondas, the Theban commander. True — Cooley: “he does not once in his whole account mention Epaminondas, architect of the Theban victory”.
  3. 3Aristotle blames Sparta’s collapse on Spartans neglecting Lycurgus’ laws. False — that is Xenophon’s line (E70). Aristotle blames the laws: “their arrangements in this were at fault” (D28).
  4. 4Aristotle says the land could support thirty thousand hoplites, yet the citizens numbered fewer than a thousand. True — “the total number was not even one thousand” (Politics 2.9, Cooley D28).
  5. 5Pausanias’ account of the whipping at Orthia is good evidence for how the ritual began. False — he repeats a local origin story (C93), and Cooley says the flogging contest “was not classical but designed for sadistic Roman tourists”.
  6. 6The Spartan mirage began only after Sparta’s defeat at Leuctra. False — Cooley: idealising texts run “from the fifth century BC onwards”, and Critias “seems to have started” it. NESA’s 2024 sample puts it after the collapse: know both.
  7. 7Tyrtaeus and Alcman were certainly Spartans. False — Cooley calls both “probably Spartan”; the Suda calls Tyrtaeus “Lakonian or Milesian”, and Crates said Alcman was from Sardis.
  8. 8Thucydides says the Lacedaemonians had kept the same constitution for more than four hundred years. True — “For four hundred years and more, until the end of the Peloponnesian War” (1.18.1, Cooley F4) — though Cooley says the system “did evolve”.

A model paragraph · written for this lesson

Xenophon is highly useful for how Sparta’s laws were meant to work in the early fourth century, but less useful as a guide to how far Spartans lived up to them. An Athenian who fought for King Agesilaus and was given an estate near Olympia, he had what Cooley calls ‘access to first hand information about Sparta and reason to be grateful to Agesilaos and Sparta’. His Constitution praises laws he dates to ‘the days of the Heraklids’ and claims that ‘everyone admires these arrangements, but no state wants to imitate them’, which makes him valuable for the ideals that Sparta and its sympathisers held; yet, in Cooley’s words, ‘The text probably depicts a somewhat idealised Sparta’. Chapter 14 cuts against his own sympathies: he admits that ‘nowadays some of them are even happy to make a display of their wealth’ and that ‘modern Lakedaimonians obey the laws of neither the gods nor Lykourgos’. An admirer’s criticism is strong evidence of change, though even this chapter assumes a perfect earlier Sparta that may never have existed. Xenophon is therefore most reliable as a well-informed sympathiser describing Spartan ideals, and most revealing where he admits that Sparta failed them.

Practice and review · 5 of 5 · chapter review · Lessons 1 and 19

Chapter review: how do we know Sparta?

  1. Why is there no ancient main source for Sparta? Apart from the poets Tyrtaeus and Alcman, no literary source originates from Sparta; the fullest accounts, Plutarch’s, were written in the Roman period; and Sparta “was not easily accessible to strangers” (Cooley).
  2. Give the dates and origin of each of the six syllabus writers. Herodotus wrote c. 450s–420s BC, Halicarnassus · Thucydides c. 455–400 BC, Athens · Xenophon c. 430–c. 350 BC, Athens · Aristotle 384–322 BC, Stagira, worked in Athens · Plutarch before AD 50 – after AD 120, Chaeronea in Boeotia · Pausanias 2nd century AD, Asia Minor (Cooley’s dates; other books differ slightly).
  3. Which writers were contemporaries of the Peloponnesian War, and which of Leuctra? The war (431–404 BC): Thucydides lived through it; Herodotus was still writing in its first years; Xenophon, born c. 430 BC, grew up during it and his Hellenika covers its last years. Leuctra (371 BC): Xenophon as an adult, Aristotle as a boy.
  4. What does Thucydides 5.68.2 reveal about the difficulty of studying Sparta? “The secret nature of their political arrangements makes it impossible to discover the numbers of Lakedaimonians”: even a careful contemporary could not find out basic facts, so he had to estimate.
  5. Why are Tyrtaeus and Alcman valuable, and what are their limitations? They are the only literary voices from inside Sparta (both probably Spartan), from the 7th century BC: Cooley calls them “Our best, and arguably only reliable literary evidence for Sparta”. But they were “writing poetry not history”, survive only in fragments, and even their birthplaces were disputed.
  6. Explain one strength and one weakness of archaeology as evidence for Sparta. Strength: it does not depend on a writer’s opinions — the Orthia lead offerings “celebrate the participants in festivals” (Cavanagh). Weakness: it is “fragmentary, biased and open to interpretation”, and dates “can seldom be refined even as closely as to a generation or two”.
  7. To what extent was the Spartan mirage created by the Spartans themselves? To a large extent: Cooley says they “played a crucial role in creating and maintaining the Spartan mirage”, Tyrtaeus sang the ideals, and the Lycurgus legend probably began in Sparta. But outsiders built it too: Herodotus, Critias, who “seems to have started” it, Xenophon, and later Plutarch’s sayings.
