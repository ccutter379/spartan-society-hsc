Four kinds of Laconian art

An archaeologist on early Sparta

The increasing prosperity of Sparta is witnessed mainly through finds from the sanctuaries: bronze tripods, elaborate pottery, iron weapons, jewellery, carved ivories.

W. G. Cavanagh, ‘Introduction to Lakonian Archaeology’, in M. G. L. Cooley (ed.), Sparta (2023), on the Early Archaic period (8th–7th centuries BC)

Where were most of these things found — and who were they for?

In sanctuaries: most were gifts to the gods. Sanctuary finds are our main evidence of Spartan prosperity in this period.

1 · Art in Archaic Sparta · We do — ivory and bone

Ivory: a luxury from far away

Two ivories from Orthia, in Cooley

A lion of the 7th century BC, only 7.5 cm long (Fig. 1) · a plaque of a ship and crew, “clumsily carved”, with one of Laconia’s earliest inscriptions — “possibly as early as the late 7th century BC” (C6)

There were no elephants in Greece. What does ivory at Orthia show?

The raw ivory was imported from the east, so Sparta was in contact — directly or through traders — with distant lands.

Who carved it: Spartiates, perioikoi or foreign craftsmen?

We do not know. The ivories were found at Orthia. Chapter 13 thinks their local subjects point to craftsmen working in Sparta; NESA’s 2021 guidelines raise the “Possibility of skilled foreign workers”.

What does a costly ivory offering say about taste and display?

Worshippers spent on small, finely carved, precious gifts — displayed to the gods at a sanctuary, not at home.

Then bone

After the mid-6th century BC carvers increasingly used bone instead of ivory, possibly because the ivory trade was interrupted (Clarke & Garner). Bone could be local, so only the ivory proves imports.

1 · Art in Archaic Sparta · I do — recall Lesson 16, then ask what bronze shows

Small bronzes: what they show about taste and daily life

A lion

The same animal as the 7th-century ivory lion and the lion inside a Laconian cup: a subject in ivory, metal and paint.

A girl

She “may be running or, since she glances back, dancing” (Cooley D64) — perhaps a fixture on a vessel. Lesson 13.

A banqueter

Reclining with a shallow dish: feasting. But it “need not, of course, show any individual diner, let alone a Spartiate one” (Cooley E71).

So what?

The banqueter comes from the tripod-support of a bronze bowl, and the girl was perhaps a fixture on a vessel or utensil (D64): costly, skilled work for people with money to spend. The Vix krater (Lesson 16) is the same craft on a huge scale: bronze-working “reached a high point” (Cavanagh). But many were found far away, so some may have been made for buyers abroad.

1 · Art in Archaic Sparta · We do — stone

Stone: reliefs, not statues

An archaeologist on Laconian sculpture

This period saw the stone hero reliefs, which have been found both in Sparta and in perioikic Lakonia; however, as in much of the Peloponnese (in contrast to Attica and other regions), free standing kouroi/korai are not attested.

W. G. Cavanagh, ‘Introduction to Lakonian Archaeology’, in Cooley (ed.), Sparta (2023), on the later Archaic period (6th–early 5th centuries BC)

What does the Chrysapha relief show?

A large seated couple, a snake behind them and tiny worshippers bringing gifts (c. 540 BC). Of a similar relief from Magoula, Cooley says: “A large snake opposite the young man suggests a heroized person” (C36).

What kind of stone sculpture is missing — and is that a Spartan oddity?

Free-standing kouroi and korai. Not just Sparta: “as in much of the Peloponnese”. Laconia’s Archaic stone sculpture was mainly relief.

What it reveals

Worship of the heroic dead, shared by Spartans and the perioikoi; the huge heroes and tiny worshippers show who mattered.

2 · Painted vases · I do — what the painters chose to paint

Painted vases: luxury, heroes — and the unexpected

An archaeologist on Laconian pottery

Lakonian pottery includes vessels for symposia, some painted with scenes of luxury, others with images of heroes, but also the unexpected, such as the cup with king Arkesilas of Kyrene in Libya weighing out silphium; great storage jars had elaborate scenes impressed from moulds.

W. G. Cavanagh, ‘Introduction to Lakonian Archaeology’, in Cooley (ed.), Sparta (2023), on the later Archaic period

When

Flourished c. 580–530 BC (textbook dates); Cooley’s examples are “Lakonian pottery of the mid-sixth century BC”; the Arkesilas cup is c. 565–560 BC.

Who

Unknown. Pots carry “no potters’ or painters’ names” (Hodkinson); “the Rider Painter” is a modern label.

For whom

Drinking parties — and buyers abroad: a hydria “found at Vulci in Italy as prized grave-goods” (Cooley Fig. 3).

What it reveals

Archaic Laconian workshops made fine drinking cups painted with luxury and heroes, for buyers at home and abroad: taste and display, not a grim austerity.

2 · Painted vases · We do — reading a painted scene

Everyday life — or myth?

Bradley calls the hunters “Spartans”. What else could they be?

Heroes of myth: painters often showed legendary hunts, and Cavanagh lists “images of heroes” among Laconian subjects.

What makes the rider more than an everyday ride?

The winged figures around him: the painter mixes the real — a horse and rider — with the divine or the imagined.

Many Laconian cups were found far from Laconia. Who were they made for?

Perhaps buyers abroad as well as Spartans. Cooley says the bronze banqueter “could equally well have been made for external consumption”; the same caution fits the cups.

So

A painted scene shows what painters and buyers liked to look at — riding, hunting, feasting, heroes. It is not a photograph of a Spartiate’s day.

3 · The austerity question · I do — the written story

Plutarch: Lycurgus drove out “useless” crafts

Source · Plutarch

[3] His next move was to expel all forms of useless or superfluous arts and crafts. The majority would have probably fallen out of use anyhow because of the new common coinage, since there was no longer a market for such skills. … nor did any craftsmen working in gold and silver ornaments.

Plutarch, Life of Lykourgos 9.3 (part) · written c. AD 100 · “his” is Lycurgus’ · trans. B. Wilson · Cooley D58

When does Plutarch place this?

Under Lycurgus — Herodotus makes him guardian of King Leobotas, whom Cooley tentatively dates c. 870–840 BC (Lesson 3). Plutarch wrote nearly a thousand years later.

Does the archaeology fit?

No. Ivories, bronzes, painted cups and reliefs flourished in the 7th and 6th centuries BC, long after Lycurgus’ supposed date. The art came before the austerity.

So the real questions are: when did Laconian art change, how sure can we be, and was austerity the cause?

3 · The austerity question · We do — Source 13.2

An archaeologist on Laconian art

Source 13.2 · W. G. Cavanagh

Already the Spartans avoid excessive monumentality at home, but the skill and grace of these artefacts serve to undermine the notion of a grim austerity.

… some argue that it is possible to recognise the onset of Spartan austerity through an apparent decline in artistic creativity in the later Archaic period, whilst others argue that in this respect the Spartans were no different from other contemporaries elsewhere in the Peloponnese and the decline in arts such as vase painting or the making of bronze vessels does not reflect a political or social reform. These and other debates continue.

W. G. Cavanagh (Professor Emeritus of Aegean Archaeology, Nottingham University), ‘Introduction to Lakonian Archaeology’, in M. G. L. Cooley (ed.), Sparta (2023): the first sentence from his section on the Early Archaic period (8th–7th centuries BC), the rest from his Conclusion · a modern summary of the excavated evidence

What does the first sentence claim?

Buildings were modest, but the early objects were skilled and graceful: not the work of a grim, austere city.

What do “some” argue?

That a decline in art in the later Archaic period marks the start of Spartan austerity.

And “others”?

That Sparta was like its Peloponnesian neighbours, so the decline need not mean a reform.

Which side does Cavanagh take?

Neither: “These and other debates continue.”

3 · The austerity question · We do — weigh the two explanations

Austerity — or a wider trend?

1 · A Spartan change
  • “some argue” (Cavanagh) that the decline marks “the onset of Spartan austerity”.
  • Stephen Hodkinson accepts a change but not a permanent one: the austere lifestyle of the classical period was, on the long view, “a temporary imposition upon a more enduring privately-oriented, wealth-based society”, and “Already by the later fifth century Sparta was being transformed back into a plutocratic society, as she had previously been before the ‘sixth-century revolution’” — one ruled by the rich. A point about institutions and wealth, not art.
2 · A wider trend
  • “others argue” (Cavanagh) that “the Spartans were no different from other contemporaries elsewhere in the Peloponnese” and that the decline “does not reflect a political or social reform”.
Either way, crafts went on

Even if crafts and arts declined after the mid-sixth century, Cooley notes that pottery, armour and weapons were still “produced locally” — probably by the perioikoi.

Cooley on the Spartan lifestyle

“comfortable prosperity masked by a display of austerity” — and “great wealth existed in Sparta in all periods”.

Where did display go?

To the gods and festivals: in Classical processions “special carriages (kannathra) were decorated with griffins and other beasts while the horses were richly caparisoned” (Cavanagh) — and to racehorses (Lesson 26).

A model summary · written for this lesson

In the 7th and 6th centuries BC Laconian craftsmen carved imported ivory (later bone), made small bronzes, painted black-figure cups and carved stone hero reliefs, most of them as offerings at sanctuaries. Their skill, their scenes of feasting, hunting, heroes and luxury, and their find-spots far from Laconia reveal a prosperous, pious and outward-looking society that spent its wealth on the gods and on fine objects rather than on grand buildings. From about the mid-6th century BC display art seems to have declined, but archaeological dates are imprecise, and scholars still debate whether this marks a Spartan turn to austerity or a trend shared across the Peloponnese.

HSC 2022 Q11(c) · I do — deconstruct the question

What the question is really asking

What do … reveal about — no glossary word

The criteria reward “a well-developed and detailed explanation”, and the feedback says better responses could “draw revelations”. So: name the object, then say what it shows and why — closest to NESA’s “Analyse”: “Draw out and relate implications.”

Art and architecture — both

Art: sculpture, painted vases, bone and ivory carving (the syllabus list). Architecture: the Amyklaion, the Menelaion and the Sanctuary of Artemis Orthia (Lesson 25).

Cultural and everyday life

Religion and festivals, feasting, hunting and riding, girls’ activity, taste and display, contacts abroad. Not the army or politics.

In Sparta in this period

To 371 BC. Most of the art is Archaic (7th–6th centuries BC), so say what changed later — and keep the hedges.

7 marks

About 12–13 minutes · four or five objects or buildings, each followed by what it reveals · about 300–400 words (our estimate) · a judgement in the first sentence.

HSC 2022 Q11(c) · annotated model, written for this lesson · paragraph 1 of 2: art

Answer first, then object → what it reveals

Art and architecture reveal a Sparta more skilled, outward-looking and devoted to the gods than the austere stereotype suggests, although its display was channelled into sanctuaries rather than grand public buildings.1 In the 7th century BC worshippers at the Sanctuary of Artemis Orthia offered carved ivories, such as a lion only 7.5 cm long, and since there were no elephants in Greece the raw ivory must have come from the east, which reveals both costly piety and links with distant lands.2 The bronze figure of a girl made in Laconia in 520–500 BC and probably found at Prizren in Kosovo, who may be running or dancing, suggests the value placed on active young women, consistent with Xenophon’s claim that Lycurgus made ‘physical fitness … as important for women as for men’.3 Laconian black-figure vases show hunting, warfare, feasting and myth, and the Arkesilas cup of c. 565–560 BC, found at Vulci in Italy, reveals both the painter’s skill and how far Laconian cups travelled.4 The thousands of cheap lead figurines offered at Orthia, which Cavanagh says ‘celebrate the participants in festivals’, suggest that worshippers of more modest means took part too.5 Yet this display did not last unchanged: figured pottery and great bronze vessels became rarer in the later 6th century BC, and archaeologists still debate whether this ‘apparent decline’ marks the onset of Spartan austerity or a trend shared across the Peloponnese.6

Then architecture — and close the loop

Architecture, by contrast, was modest — ‘the temple of Orthia measured about 16 x 7 m’ (Cavanagh, in Cooley) — which supports Thucydides’ point that Sparta lacked extravagant public buildings.7 Yet the late-6th-century throne of Apollo at Amyklai and the Menelaion, a shrine to Helen and Menelaus beside Mycenaean remains, show Spartans investing in sanctuaries that tied the city to its heroic past.8 Overall, art and architecture reveal a society that spent its wealth above all on the gods and on display at festivals rather than on grand public buildings, and whose Archaic taste for fine objects sits uneasily with the later image of Spartan austerity.9

Practice and review · 2 of 6 · true, false, justify · written for this lesson

True or false? Justify with a source

  1. 1The ivory carved for Spartan sanctuaries came from Greek elephants. False — there were no elephants in Greece; the raw ivory was imported from the east.
  2. 2Cavanagh says free-standing kouroi and korai are not attested in Laconia. True — “as in much of the Peloponnese”; Laconia’s Archaic stone sculpture was mainly relief.
  3. 3All three textbooks agree that the goddess plaque from Orthia is ivory. False — ivory (Clarke & Garner), ivory or bone (Bradley), bone (Hurley et al.).
  4. 4Laconian painters signed their vases. False — there are “no potters’ or painters’ names” (Hodkinson); names like the Rider Painter are modern labels.
  5. 5Cavanagh says some Laconian pottery was painted with scenes of luxury. True — “vessels for symposia, some painted with scenes of luxury, others with images of heroes”.
  6. 6Plutarch says Lycurgus expelled useless arts and crafts. True — “His next move was to expel all forms of useless or superfluous arts and crafts” (D58) — but he wrote c. AD 100, and the art flourished long after Lycurgus’ supposed date.
  7. 7Cavanagh is sure that a Spartan reform caused the decline of Laconian art. False — “some argue” it was austerity, “others argue” it was not: “These and other debates continue.”
  8. 8Laconia stopped making pottery after the 6th century BC. False — goods were still made locally, “for example pottery and of course armour and weapons” (Cooley); Hodkinson mentions classical Laconian red-figure pottery.

A model paragraph · written for this lesson

Laconian art supports the view that something changed in the later Archaic period, but it does not prove that Sparta turned austere. Carved ivories, black-figure cups such as the Arkesilas cup of c. 565–560 BC and great bronze vessels such as the Vix krater show highly skilled craftsmen until the later 6th century BC, after which figured pottery and great bronze vessels became rarer, and, according to Clarke and Garner, bone increasingly replaced ivory. Some archaeologists, as Cavanagh reports, read this ‘apparent decline in artistic creativity’ as ‘the onset of Spartan austerity’. Yet archaeological dates ‘can seldom be refined even as closely as to a generation or two’, and others argue that the Spartans were ‘no different from other contemporaries elsewhere in the Peloponnese’. Crafts also went on: Cooley notes that pottery and ‘of course armour and weapons’ were still made locally, and Euonyma dedicated a bronze mirror to Athena in the mid-5th century BC. Laconian art therefore suggests that Spartan display narrowed after the mid-6th century BC, but whether austerity caused that change remains, in Cavanagh’s words, one of the debates that continue.

Practice and review · 6 of 6 · the Quick Check for Chapter 13, sections 1–2 (1–4), and two more (5–6) · answers written for this lesson

Review: art and the austerity question

  1. Why does ivory found at Sparta show that Sparta traded with distant lands? Ivory comes from elephants, and there were none in Greece, so the raw ivory must have been imported from the east — contact with distant lands, directly or through traders. (Who carved it is unknown: the ivories were found at Orthia.)
  2. Name one example each of Spartan sculpture, painted pottery and ivory or bone carving. Sculpture: the Chrysapha hero relief (c. 540 BC) or the bronze girl (520–500 BC). Pottery: the Arkesilas cup (c. 565–560 BC). Ivory: the 7th-century ivory lion from Orthia (Cooley Fig. 1).
  3. What kind of stone sculpture did Laconia produce, and what kind is missing? Hero reliefs, found in Sparta and in perioikic Laconia; free-standing kouroi and korai are “not attested” — “as in much of the Peloponnese”.
  4. Give the two explanations for the decline of display art in the later 6th century BC. Some argue it marks “the onset of Spartan austerity”; others argue the Spartans were “no different from other contemporaries elsewhere in the Peloponnese”, so it “does not reflect a political or social reform” (Cavanagh).
  5. How sure can historians be about when Laconian art declined? Not very: Cooley hedges it (“Even if”); “an apparent decline … in the later Archaic period” is how “some” scholars read the evidence; and archaeological dates “can seldom be refined even as closely as to a generation or two”.
  6. What can a painted hunt on a Laconian cup tell us about everyday life — and what can it not? That hunting was a subject painters and buyers valued; not whether Spartiates hunted that way, since it may be a hunt from myth and the cup may have been made for buyers abroad.
